A founder reached out with a simple ask: "We need another developer. Our ops team can't keep up with order volume."

That's a reasonable-sounding request. It's also, more often than not, the wrong diagnosis, so before scoping a hire, I asked to sit in on how an order actually moved from "received" to "fulfilled."

## What was actually happening

The order came in through one system. Someone manually copied the details into a spreadsheet. Someone else checked the spreadsheet against inventory, by hand, a few times a day. If stock was low, a third person had to notice and re-order. Nothing was connected. Three humans were the glue holding four systems together.

The team wasn't short on developers. They were short on a system that didn't need three people acting as human middleware for every single order.

> Three humans were the glue holding four systems together.

## What we built instead

Instead of a new hire, we built the pricing and inventory logic directly into one platform: orders synced automatically, stock levels updated in real time, and reordering triggered itself below a threshold instead of waiting for someone to notice. The spreadsheet disappeared entirely.

## The outcome

The "capacity problem" turned out to be a thirty-minute manual reconciliation happening four times a day, multiplied across a growing order volume. Once that step was automated, the same team that was drowning had headroom again, without adding a single person.

The lesson I keep relearning: when someone asks for more hands, it's worth spending a day watching what those hands are actually doing before agreeing.
