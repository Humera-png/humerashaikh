There's a specific moment I look for when a founder describes their bottleneck: do they say "we don't have enough people to do X," or do they say "X takes forever and nobody enjoys doing it"?

Those sound similar. They're not the same problem.

## Headcount problems

A real headcount problem looks like: demand for a well-defined, already-efficient piece of work has outgrown the number of people doing it. The process itself is fine. There's just more of it than your current team can get through.

## Systems problems dressed as headcount problems

Far more often, what I actually find is a process nobody has questioned in a while: a spreadsheet that should be a database, an approval that should be a rule, a status update that should be automatic instead of someone typing it into Slack every morning.

Adding a person to that process doesn't remove the friction. It just means the friction now costs two salaries instead of one.

## How to tell which one you have

Ask: if I doubled the team tomorrow, would this actually get faster, or would I just have twice as many people doing the same manual thing?

If the honest answer is the second one, you don't need more people yet. You need the process rebuilt so that the people you already have stop being the bottleneck.
